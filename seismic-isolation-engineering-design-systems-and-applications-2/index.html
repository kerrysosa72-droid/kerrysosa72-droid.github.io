Seismic Isolation Engineering: Design, Systems, and Applications

anitaa October 1, 2026

Seismic isolation engineering is a specialized approach to earthquake-resistant structural design that modifies how a building or structure responds to ground motion. Rather than relying entirely on the strength, stiffness, and ductility of the superstructure to absorb earthquake effects, a seismic isolation system introduces a controlled interface between the supporting substructure and the isolated structure. Properly designed bearings or sliding devices can permit controlled horizontal movement, alter the effective dynamic properties of the structure, and provide energy-dissipation mechanisms that influence seismic response.

The engineering challenge is not simply selecting a bearing capable of supporting a building. A complete seismic isolation design must account for vertical load capacity, lateral stiffness, effective damping, displacement demand, structural period, foundation behavior, stability, bearing deformation, connection design, and the movement requirements of architectural and MEP systems. The isolation interface also has to remain compatible with seismic gaps, flexible utility connections, equipment anchorage, and other nonstructural components.

For U.S. projects, seismic isolation must be evaluated within the applicable structural and regulatory framework. ASCE 7, the International Building Code (IBC), California Building Code (CBC), project specifications, jurisdictional requirements, and healthcare requirements where applicable can all influence design and approval. For qualifying California healthcare facilities, HCAI requirements and the legacy OSHPD framework may introduce additional coordination considerations.

seismic isolation engineering therefore sits at the intersection of structural analysis, dynamic response, material behavior, construction detailing, and multidisciplinary coordination. Whether the project involves a new commercial building, hospital, critical facility, industrial structure, or seismic retrofit, the isolation system should be selected from calculated project requirements rather than from a generic product category.

What Is Seismic Isolation Engineering?

Seismic isolation engineering involves designing the structural interface and associated components that modify a structure's response to earthquake ground motion. The central concept is to introduce controlled flexibility between the ground-supported substructure and the isolated superstructure. This can shift the dynamic characteristics of the building and, depending on the selected system and ground motion, influence transmitted forces, accelerations, and deformation demands.

Seismic Isolation vs. Conventional Seismic Design

Conventional seismic design generally develops resistance through structural strength, stiffness, ductility, and energy dissipation within the structural system. A moment frame, shear wall, braced frame, or combination of these systems is designed to carry gravity and lateral demands while maintaining an acceptable response during the design earthquake.

Seismic base isolation adds another dynamic mechanism. Isolation bearings or sliding devices are intentionally designed to accommodate lateral movement at a defined isolation plane. This allows engineers to separate some of the ground motion characteristics from the response of the superstructure. The objective is not to eliminate earthquake forces but to modify the way those forces are transmitted and distributed.

How Base Isolation Changes Structural Response

An isolation system commonly increases the effective fundamental period of the isolated structure compared with a fixed-base configuration. The resulting response depends on the relationship between the modified period and the characteristics of the design ground motion. Energy dissipation through elastomeric hysteresis, lead cores, friction, or other mechanisms can further influence response.

This creates an important engineering tradeoff: reducing certain acceleration and force demands can be accompanied by increased horizontal movement at the isolation interface. The design must therefore provide adequate displacement capacity and clearance while maintaining vertical support and stability.

Source, Structure, and Isolation Interface

The complete load path extends from the ground motion into the foundation, substructure, isolation bearings, superstructure, and connected systems. Bearing reactions must be transferred through structural elements and connections without creating unintended weak points.

For that reason, seismic isolation engineering cannot be treated as an isolated bearing-selection exercise. It requires structural seismic analysis, foundation coordination, connection detailing, movement accommodation, and multidisciplinary review. This integrated approach provides the foundation for evaluating specific isolation technologies and their suitability for a project.

How Seismic Isolation Systems Work

A seismic isolation system has to perform several functions simultaneously. It must carry gravity loads, accommodate earthquake-induced horizontal movement, provide predictable dynamic characteristics, and remain stable under the required load combinations. These requirements make the mechanical behavior of an isolation system more complex than the simple concept of placing flexible bearings beneath a structure.

Vertical Load and Horizontal Movement

Most isolation bearings are designed to carry substantial vertical compression while permitting controlled lateral deformation or movement. Laminated elastomeric bearings, for example, use alternating layers of elastomer and steel reinforcement to provide vertical load capacity while allowing horizontal shear deformation. Sliding systems use a low-friction interface to permit relative movement while maintaining vertical support.

The vertical and lateral characteristics have to be evaluated together. Bearing geometry, compression, shear deformation, overturning effects, and support conditions can influence stability and performance.

Effective Stiffness and Damping

Engineers evaluate effective stiffness because an isolation system does not necessarily behave like a simple linear spring. Elastomeric bearings may exhibit nonlinear hysteretic behavior, while sliding systems are influenced by friction characteristics and, in some configurations, restoring mechanisms.

Effective damping represents energy dissipation in the isolation system and is an important parameter in response calculations. The appropriate values depend on the actual system, loading history, temperature, frequency, displacement, and applicable design methodology.

Natural Period and Dynamic Response

The isolation system changes the combined dynamic properties of the structure. Engineers therefore consider the effective period, structural modes, response spectrum characteristics, damping, and interaction between the isolation system and superstructure.

A properly engineered system seeks meaningful period separation and controlled response rather than simply maximizing flexibility. Excessive flexibility, insufficient restoring behavior, inadequate stability, or poorly characterized properties can create undesirable response characteristics.

Seismic Displacement

One of the most important consequences of isolation is movement. An isolated structure may experience substantial displacement relative to its foundation during an earthquake. The design must therefore accommodate that movement through seismic gaps, moat spaces, flexible utility connections, expansion joints, architectural interfaces, and appropriately detailed structural connections.

The isolation system is successful only when the entire building can accommodate the calculated movement. Bearings, foundations, utilities, finishes, and restraints must operate as one coordinated system.

Types of Seismic Isolation Bearings and Systems

Seismic isolation systems are available in several configurations, each based on different mechanical principles. Selection should be driven by structural demand, vertical reactions, displacement, dynamic characteristics, environmental conditions, geometry, inspection requirements, and project objectives rather than by a universal preference for one technology.

Elastomeric Seismic Bearings

Laminated elastomeric bearings use alternating layers of rubber and steel reinforcement. The elastomer provides lateral flexibility through shear deformation, while the reinforcing plates limit excessive lateral bulging under compression and contribute to vertical load capacity.

Natural rubber, synthetic rubber, and specialized elastomeric compounds can be formulated for different applications. Bearing geometry and material properties affect stiffness, stability, and deformation capacity.

Lead-Rubber and High-Damping Rubber Bearings

Lead-rubber bearings incorporate a lead core that contributes energy dissipation while the surrounding laminated elastomer provides vertical support and lateral flexibility. High-damping rubber bearings use elastomer formulations with greater inherent damping characteristics.

These systems require engineering evaluation of their effective properties over the relevant range of displacement and loading conditions. Nominal bearing dimensions alone do not establish dynamic equivalence between different systems.

Sliding and Friction Pendulum Systems

Sliding seismic isolation bearings permit movement through a controlled sliding interface. Friction characteristics affect energy dissipation, while friction pendulum systems use a curved surface to produce pendulum-type restoring behavior.

Temperature, pressure, velocity, surface condition, material compatibility, and long-term environmental exposure can affect sliding behavior. These factors become especially important for projects with demanding service environments.

Roller and Ball Bearings

Roller and ball systems can provide controlled movement through rolling interfaces where the application supports their use. Their design requires consideration of vertical load, displacement, stability, alignment, rolling behavior, restraint, and inspection.

Seismic Expansion Joints

Regardless of bearing technology, the building must accommodate movement at interfaces. Seismic expansion joints can be essential where structural separation or isolation-plane movement crosses architectural or building-envelope assemblies.

The correct isolation solution is therefore a system rather than a single component: bearings, supports, connections, movement joints, utility interfaces, and structural elements must all work within the calculated displacement envelope.

Seismic Isolation Design: Loads, Displacement, and Dynamic Behavior

Seismic isolation design begins with understanding the seismic demand and determining how the proposed isolation system changes the structural response. The analysis must connect site-specific seismic criteria, structural properties, isolation-system behavior, and displacement requirements.

Seismic Demand and Base Shear

The starting point includes the applicable seismic hazard, site characteristics, design ground motion, structural configuration, and project-specific criteria. Engineers then evaluate how the isolated system affects response quantities such as base shear, acceleration, and deformation.

Seismic isolation may reduce certain demands transmitted into the superstructure, but the magnitude of any change is project-dependent. Ground-motion characteristics, effective period, damping, structural modes, and isolation properties all influence the resulting response.

Lateral Stiffness and Effective Damping

Effective stiffness and damping are fundamental design parameters. Elastomeric systems derive their behavior from rubber properties, geometry, reinforcing layers, and deformation. Sliding systems depend strongly on friction and the mechanics of their sliding and restoring components.

These characteristics should be established using appropriate engineering data and design procedures. Generic assumptions can lead to inaccurate estimates of period or displacement.

Vertical Load Capacity

Each bearing location has to support its applicable gravity and seismic load combinations. Engineers consider dead loads, live loads, equipment loads, overturning effects, load redistribution, and compression stability.

Bearing reactions also determine the requirements for bearing plates, pedestals, anchor plates, concrete supports, and structural steel. The supporting structure must be capable of transferring the calculated forces without local failure or excessive deformation.

Horizontal Displacement and Clearance

Displacement is often the defining geometric constraint in an isolated structure. The isolation plane must provide adequate clearance for calculated movement while preventing unintended contact with surrounding structural or architectural elements.

Seismic gaps must be coordinated with stairs, elevators, façades, partitions, fire-rated assemblies, utilities, waterproofing, and mechanical systems. A structurally adequate bearing arrangement can still encounter performance problems if the surrounding building cannot accommodate its movement.

For this reason, seismic isolator calculations should be integrated with structural and architectural coordination from the earliest design stages.

Structural, Foundation, and MEP Considerations

An isolation system transfers its behavior into every component connected to the isolation plane. Structural supports, foundations, utilities, equipment, and architectural elements therefore need to be designed around the expected movement rather than treated as independent disciplines.

Isolation Plane and Foundations

The location of the isolation plane affects construction, access, structural load paths, and utility coordination. In a new building, the isolation interface can be incorporated into the structural concept from the beginning. This allows foundation elements, bearing pedestals, structural diaphragms, and access provisions to be developed together.

Foundation design must consider the bearing reactions and the underlying soil and geotechnical conditions. Settlement, differential movement, foundation capacity, and soil-structure interaction assumptions can influence the feasibility and performance of an isolation system.

Bearing Supports and Connections

Bearing plates, anchor plates, anchor bolts, pedestals, structural steel, and concrete interfaces must transfer vertical and lateral forces reliably. Local stresses around bearing supports may require detailed structural analysis rather than simple load-rating comparisons.

Custom support assemblies can become necessary when standard bearing geometry does not align with the structural grid, existing foundations, equipment configuration, or available installation space.

MEP Systems Across the Isolation Plane

Piping, ductwork, conduit, cable trays, fire protection, and other services crossing the isolation interface must accommodate relative movement. Rigid connections can restrict intended bearing movement and create unintended force paths.

Flexible piping connections, appropriately detailed HVAC interfaces, seismic expansion joints, and movement-compatible electrical systems should therefore be coordinated with the calculated displacement envelope.

New Construction and Retrofit

New construction generally provides more opportunity to integrate the isolation interface into the structural and architectural concept. Retrofit projects are more complex because existing foundations, utilities, structural conditions, access limitations, and occupied spaces may constrain the available isolation strategy.

A retrofit may require temporary shoring, foundation modifications, structural strengthening, utility relocation, and phased installation. Feasibility should be established through investigation and analysis before procurement or fabrication begins.

Seismic Isolation for Commercial, Healthcare, and Critical Facilities

Seismic isolation is considered for a range of structures where project-specific performance objectives justify modifying the structural response. It is particularly relevant when acceleration, operational continuity, structural damage control, or protection of sensitive contents is an important consideration.

Commercial and Institutional Buildings

Office buildings, institutional structures, high-value commercial facilities, and other occupied buildings may be evaluated for seismic isolation when conventional structural design does not align with the desired performance objectives or when specific project conditions make isolation attractive.

The decision depends on the complete structural system, site hazard, building configuration, cost and constructability, architectural requirements, and expected performance.

Hospitals and Healthcare Facilities

Hospitals present additional challenges because structural performance is only one part of seismic resilience. Medical equipment, emergency systems, piping, HVAC, electrical distribution, communications, and other nonstructural components may be essential to continued operation.

For applicable California healthcare projects, HCAI requirements and the relevant regulatory framework must be incorporated into design and review. The former OSHPD designation remains familiar in the industry, but current projects should be evaluated under the applicable HCAI requirements and governing code edition.

Critical Facilities and Sensitive Environments

Emergency facilities, government buildings, laboratories, research centers, data centers, semiconductor facilities, and precision manufacturing environments can have demanding performance objectives. Structural acceleration may be relevant not only to structural components but also to equipment and operational continuity.

Seismic isolation does not automatically provide equipment protection. Sensitive equipment may still require dedicated anchorage, seismic restraints, vibration-control systems, or flexible connections.

The engineering objective should therefore be defined at the project level. Isolation is one potential part of a broader performance-based strategy rather than an automatic requirement for every critical facility.

Codes, Standards, and the Seismic Isolation Engineering Workflow

Seismic isolation engineering must be developed within the applicable U.S. regulatory and technical framework. Codes establish minimum requirements, but successful design also depends on project-specific criteria, structural analysis, specifications, geotechnical information, owner requirements, and jurisdictional review.

ASCE 7 and Building Codes

ASCE/SEI 7 provides important seismic design provisions for structures and nonstructural components, including provisions applicable to seismic isolation where adopted and applicable to the project. The IBC establishes the broader building-code framework, while the CBC governs qualifying California projects under the applicable edition.

Engineers must confirm the specific edition, amendments, occupancy classification, site parameters, structural system, and jurisdictional requirements rather than treating a code reference as a universal design solution.

HCAI and OSHPD

Healthcare projects in California may be subject to HCAI requirements and specialized review procedures. OSHPD is the former name associated with California's healthcare construction regulatory framework and remains common terminology in project documentation and industry discussions.

A statement that a component is “OSHPD approved” or “HCAI pre-approved” should not be interpreted as a blanket approval for every application. Project-specific structural calculations, installation details, connections, and applicable requirements still matter.

ACI, AISC, and Supporting Design Requirements

ACI provisions can become relevant to concrete foundations, pedestals, bearing supports, and anchorage. AISC provisions may apply to structural steel supporting frames, plates, connections, and fabricated assemblies. These standards operate alongside seismic design requirements rather than replacing the isolation analysis.

From Analysis to Installation

A practical seismic isolation workflow begins with project seismic criteria and performance objectives. Engineers then review architectural, structural, geotechnical, and MEP information; establish the isolation plane; calculate vertical reactions and displacement demands; evaluate stiffness, damping, friction, and bearing behavior; and compare appropriate isolation technologies.

The process continues through bearing layout, foundation and support design, seismic gaps, MEP movement coordination, seismic restraint analysis, BIM/CAD development, fabrication, installation, inspection, and verification.

This continuity is important because fabrication geometry must reflect the assumptions used in engineering calculations. A change to bearing dimensions, support location, connection configuration, or restraint arrangement can alter the system and may require engineering review.

Seismic Isolation, Seismic Restraint, BIM, and Custom Fabrication

Structural isolation and nonstructural seismic protection are related but distinct engineering functions. A building may use a structural isolation system while still requiring seismic bracing and anchorage for mechanical, electrical, and architectural components.

Seismic Restraint and Equipment Protection

MEP systems, HVAC equipment, piping, ductwork, conduit, cable trays, and other nonstructural components may require seismic restraints under applicable project provisions. Equipment may also require anchorage independent of the building's structural isolation system.

Restraints must be coordinated with the movement allowed by the isolation plane. A rigid connection that unintentionally bridges an isolation interface can restrict movement or create a vibration or seismic bypass path.

Structural Engineering and Seismic Calculations

Seismic calculations establish the technical basis for bearing reactions, structural response, displacement, support requirements, and connections. Structural engineering also determines whether foundations, slabs, steel framing, concrete members, and bearing supports can accommodate the calculated demands.

This is where seismic isolation engineering becomes substantially different from purchasing an off-the-shelf bearing. The component has to function within a calculated structural system.

BIM and 3D CAD Coordination

BIM 3D CAD modeling can help structural and MEP teams coordinate bearing locations, clearances, utility crossings, structural supports, access areas, and movement zones before fabrication. This is particularly valuable where large displacement requirements interact with crowded mechanical rooms, architectural finishes, or complex structural geometry.

Custom Fabrication

Custom bearing plates, support frames, pedestals, brackets, structural steel assemblies, equipment mounts, and custom strut channels may be required when standard components do not fit the project geometry.

Material selection can include carbon steel, structural steel, stainless steel, aluminum, or higher-strength and alloy steels depending on load, environment, fabrication requirements, and specifications. Galvanizing, powder coating, or other protective finishes may be appropriate for corrosion resistance or project-specific environmental conditions.

How to Select a Seismic Isolation System

Selecting a seismic isolation system is an engineering decision that should begin with measurable project requirements. A bearing's vertical load rating is only one part of the selection process.

Define Performance Objectives

First determine what the isolation strategy is intended to accomplish. Objectives may include reducing structural acceleration, modifying seismic force demand, controlling damage, supporting operational continuity, protecting sensitive contents, or achieving a combination of performance goals.

Establish Loads and Displacement

Engineers should identify gravity loads, seismic load combinations, bearing reactions, load distribution, overturning effects, and required horizontal displacement. The displacement requirement must then be translated into physical clearance at the isolation plane and flexibility requirements for connected systems.

Compare Technologies

Elastomeric, high-damping, lead-rubber, sliding, friction pendulum, roller, and other isolation systems have different mechanical characteristics. Comparison should consider effective stiffness, damping, friction, restoring force, stability, deformation capacity, vertical load, and environmental behavior.

Evaluate the Complete Building Interface

Foundation capacity, bearing alignment, structural connections, architectural movement joints, MEP flexibility, equipment anchorage, and seismic restraints all influence system suitability.

Environmental exposure also matters. Temperature, moisture, ultraviolet exposure, chemicals, saltwater, corrosion, and inspection access can affect material and finish selection, particularly in industrial, marine, or infrastructure applications.

Specify Measurable Requirements

Procurement documents should identify bearing performance requirements rather than relying exclusively on a product name. Relevant information can include vertical reactions, displacement capacity, effective stiffness, effective damping, friction characteristics where applicable, dimensions, environmental conditions, attachment requirements, seismic gaps, inspection criteria, and installation tolerances.

This approach gives structural engineers, contractors, owners, and procurement teams a defensible basis for evaluating isolation systems while allowing qualified manufacturers and fabricators to develop solutions that meet the actual project requirements.

Common Seismic Isolation Engineering Mistakes

Even a technically sound isolation concept can be compromised by poor coordination or incomplete assumptions. One common mistake is selecting bearings solely according to vertical load capacity. A bearing can support the required gravity load while still having unsuitable lateral stiffness, damping, displacement capacity, stability, or environmental characteristics.

Ignoring seismic displacement is another major problem. Isolation systems permit movement by design, so insufficient clearance can result in contact with surrounding structures, finishes, utilities, or equipment. Rigid piping, conduit, ductwork, or other connections can also unintentionally bridge the isolation interface and prevent the structure from moving as intended.

Dynamic properties require equal attention. Effective stiffness, damping, friction, restoring behavior, and deformation characteristics influence seismic response. Treating different elastomeric or sliding systems as dynamically interchangeable can produce inaccurate analytical assumptions.

Retrofit projects introduce additional risks. Existing foundations, hidden utilities, structural deterioration, access restrictions, and temporary support requirements should be investigated before an isolation strategy is finalized. Fabricating supports before structural geometry is resolved can create expensive field modifications.

Another frequent error is treating structural seismic isolation as a substitute for seismic restraint. MEP systems and equipment may still require anchorage and bracing, and those restraints must be compatible with the permitted structural movement.

Finally, installation and verification cannot be separated from design. Bearing alignment, support elevations, connection geometry, clearances, and movement provisions should be checked against approved engineering documents. A technically appropriate system can underperform if installation conditions differ materially from the assumptions used in the calculations.

How to Specify Seismic Isolation Systems for U.S. Projects

A strong specification gives the design team and procurement professionals enough information to evaluate performance without reducing the selection process to a product name. Project documentation should begin with the location, jurisdiction, occupancy, applicable code edition, seismic criteria, and owner performance objectives.

The structural portion should identify the proposed isolation-plane location, structural configuration, bearing reactions, vertical load combinations, expected horizontal displacement, effective stiffness requirements, effective damping assumptions, and any applicable stability or deformation criteria. For sliding systems, friction characteristics and relevant environmental conditions should also be addressed.

Support and connection documents should define bearing geometry, structural attachment, anchor requirements, support plates, pedestals, steel assemblies, concrete interfaces, and required installation tolerances. Seismic gaps and displacement clearances should be coordinated with architecture, waterproofing, fire protection, elevators, stairs, façades, and other building components.

MEP specifications should identify where utilities cross the isolation interface and establish movement requirements for piping, HVAC, ductwork, conduit, cable trays, and other systems. Seismic restraint requirements should be coordinated rather than developed independently.

Environmental requirements may include temperature range, moisture exposure, corrosion protection, chemicals, marine conditions, ultraviolet exposure, and required protective coatings. Galvanizing, powder coating, stainless steel, or other materials may be selected according to the project environment.

Inspection, testing, documentation, BIM/CAD coordination, fabrication drawings, installation procedures, and maintenance provisions should also be defined where applicable.

FAQ: Seismic Isolation Engineering

What is seismic isolation engineering?

Seismic isolation engineering is the structural discipline of designing an isolation interface that modifies how a building or structure responds to earthquake ground motion. Engineers evaluate seismic demand, vertical loads, displacement, effective stiffness, damping, bearing behavior, foundations, structural connections, and building movement. The objective is to develop a coordinated system in which the isolation components and supporting structure work together under the applicable seismic design criteria.

How does seismic base isolation work?

Seismic base isolation places controlled flexibility and energy dissipation between the supporting ground or substructure and the isolated structure. Bearings can carry gravity loads while permitting horizontal movement during an earthquake. By changing the effective dynamic characteristics of the structure, the isolation system can alter transmitted acceleration and force demands. The actual response depends on ground motion, building properties, bearing behavior, damping, stiffness, and displacement.

What are seismic isolation bearings?

Seismic isolation bearings are structural components designed to support vertical loads while permitting controlled lateral movement. Common systems include laminated elastomeric bearings, high-damping rubber bearings, lead-rubber bearings, sliding bearings, and friction pendulum systems. Selection depends on calculated vertical reactions, seismic displacement, effective stiffness, damping, stability, environmental exposure, structural geometry, and project-specific performance requirements.

What is the difference between elastomeric and sliding seismic isolation bearings?

Elastomeric bearings generally accommodate lateral movement through shear deformation of rubber or elastomeric layers reinforced with steel plates. Sliding systems permit movement through a controlled sliding interface and may use friction and restoring mechanisms to influence response. Their behavior is fundamentally different, so engineers evaluate each technology according to the project's load, displacement, dynamic, stability, environmental, and maintenance requirements.

Why is displacement so important in seismic isolation design?

An isolated structure needs room to move during an earthquake. Consequently, seismic isolation design must address calculated horizontal displacement as well as bearing capacity. Seismic gaps, flexible piping, HVAC connections, electrical services, expansion joints, façades, stairs, elevators, and other interfaces must accommodate the expected movement. Insufficient clearance can create unintended contact or force paths that compromise the isolation concept.

Does seismic isolation eliminate earthquake forces?

No. Seismic isolation does not eliminate earthquake forces or ground motion. It changes the dynamic relationship between the ground and the isolated structure. Depending on the project and ground motion, isolation can modify structural acceleration, base shear, deformation, and energy dissipation. Engineers must still design the structure, foundations, bearings, connections, and nonstructural components for the applicable seismic demands.

Is seismic isolation suitable for hospitals?

Seismic isolation may be considered for hospitals when it supports the project's structural and operational performance objectives. Healthcare facilities can have stringent requirements for structural systems, medical equipment, utilities, HVAC, electrical systems, and other nonstructural components. Applicable HCAI requirements should be evaluated for qualifying California healthcare projects. Isolation should be considered as part of an integrated seismic strategy rather than assumed to be appropriate for every hospital.

Can seismic isolation be used for existing buildings?

Yes, seismic isolation can be evaluated as a retrofit strategy for certain existing structures, but feasibility is highly project-specific. Engineers may need to investigate foundations, existing framing, building condition, utilities, access, structural geometry, available clearance, and construction sequencing. Installation can require temporary shoring, foundation modifications, structural strengthening, and utility relocation. A detailed existing-building assessment is therefore essential before selecting a retrofit approach.

Do isolated buildings still need seismic bracing?

Structural seismic isolation does not automatically eliminate seismic bracing and anchorage requirements for nonstructural components. HVAC equipment, piping, ductwork, conduit, cable trays, equipment, and other systems may still require seismic restraint under applicable project requirements. Those restraints must be coordinated with the movement permitted by the isolation system so that they do not unintentionally create rigid connections across the isolation interface.

How do HVAC and piping systems cross an isolation plane?

Utilities crossing an isolation plane need to accommodate the expected relative movement between the isolated structure and supporting substructure. Depending on the application, this can involve flexible piping connections, flexible duct connections, seismic expansion joints, movement-compatible electrical systems, and appropriately detailed supports. The required solution depends on displacement, pressure, temperature, utility type, seismic requirements, and the geometry of the isolation interface.

What information is needed to select seismic isolation bearings?

Important information includes project seismic criteria, structural configuration, vertical loads, bearing reactions, load combinations, horizontal displacement, isolation-plane geometry, effective stiffness and damping requirements, environmental conditions, support details, and applicable code requirements. Engineers should also consider foundation conditions, seismic gaps, MEP interfaces, architectural movement, equipment anchorage, inspection access, and installation tolerances.
