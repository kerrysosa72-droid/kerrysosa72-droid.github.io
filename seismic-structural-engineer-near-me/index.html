What Does a Seismic Structural Engineer Do?

Seismic Analysis and Structural Design

A seismic structural engineer evaluates how a building or structural system responds to earthquake demands and develops an engineering approach that addresses those demands. The work can include seismic load determination, structural analysis, lateral-system evaluation, member design, connection design, foundation considerations, structural detailing, and preparation or review of engineering documentation.

Seismic engineering is not simply a matter of applying one horizontal force to a building. Earthquake effects interact with the building's mass, geometry, stiffness, strength, ductility, structural configuration, site conditions, and intended use. The engineer must understand how seismic demand is distributed through the structure and how forces move from floors and roofs into the lateral-force-resisting system and ultimately into the foundations and supporting soil.

Depending on the project, the structural system may include moment frames, special moment frames, concentrically or eccentrically braced frames, reinforced concrete shear walls, structural steel framing, or combinations of these systems. Floor and roof diaphragms also play an important role in transferring lateral forces to the designated resisting elements.

A seismic engineering consultant may also become involved with existing-building assessment, structural strengthening, equipment anchorage, nonstructural seismic components, or retrofit design. For a contractor, this can mean reviewing field conditions and developing details that can actually be constructed. For an architect, it can mean coordinating structural requirements with architectural layouts. For an owner, it can mean understanding whether an existing facility has sufficient capacity for a planned modification.

The Sigma Source's structural engineering approach connects analysis with documentation and project execution. Seismic calculations, structural drawings, BIM models, connection details, support designs, and fabricated components should represent the same engineering intent rather than functioning as disconnected project deliverables.

Structural Load Paths and Seismic Forces

One of the most important concepts in seismic structural engineering is the load path. Earthquake-induced forces must have a continuous and rational route through the building. If a diaphragm, connection, brace, wall, beam, column, base plate, anchor, or foundation cannot transfer its required demand, the overall load path may be compromised.

That is why a qualified seismic design engineer near me evaluates relationships between structural components rather than treating each component independently. Connection capacity, anchorage, diaphragm behavior, collector elements, and foundation response can be just as important as the strength of primary framing members.

How Seismic Structural Engineering Addresses Earthquake Loads

Lateral-Force-Resisting Systems

A central responsibility of seismic analysis and design is determining how the building resists lateral earthquake forces. Lateral-force-resisting systems can include moment frames, braced frames, shear walls, or combinations of structural systems.

The engineer evaluates how these systems interact with diaphragms and vertical structural elements. Story drift, member demand, stiffness, strength, stability, and connection behavior can all influence the design. The selected system must be compatible with the building's geometry and intended use.

For example, a warehouse with large open floor areas may have different structural and seismic considerations than a hospital, high-rise office building, manufacturing plant, or data center. Industrial facilities may also contain heavy equipment, elevated platforms, piping, tanks, and other components that introduce additional structural and nonstructural seismic considerations.

Diaphragms, Frames, and Shear Walls

Floor and roof diaphragms transfer seismic forces toward the vertical elements that resist those forces. Their behavior must therefore be considered as part of the overall structural system. Collector elements, chords, connections, and diaphragm attachments may become important components of the force-transfer mechanism.

Moment frames rely on frame action between beams and columns, while braced frames use diagonal members to resist lateral forces. Shear walls transfer lateral forces through reinforced concrete or other structural wall systems. Each approach has different detailing, stiffness, ductility, connection, and foundation implications.

Structural Connections and Foundations

A structural analysis is only useful if the resulting forces can be transferred through actual connections and into the foundations. Beam-to-column connections, brace connections, base plates, anchor bolts, reinforcing steel, concrete anchorage, and foundation elements must be considered as part of the complete system.

For this reason, seismic calculations for buildings should connect directly to engineering drawings and construction details. A connection that appears adequate at the member level may still require evaluation of bolt strength, welds, plates, concrete breakout, anchor capacity, or supporting structural elements.

ASCE 7, IBC, CBC, and Applicable Seismic Requirements

ASCE 7 is a central reference for determining structural loads and seismic design criteria in many U.S. projects. Its application depends on the applicable code framework and project conditions. Seismic design can involve parameters related to ground motion, building characteristics, structural systems, occupancy, and response.

An ASCE 7 seismic engineer must still evaluate the complete applicable regulatory framework rather than treating ASCE 7 as a standalone requirement.

California Building Code

For California projects, the California Building Code (CBC) works within the state's broader regulatory structure and incorporates referenced standards. Local jurisdictions can also have amendments and project-specific requirements.

A California seismic project should therefore be evaluated according to the applicable CBC edition, local requirements, project scope, building use, and AHJ expectations.

ACI, AISC, HCAI, and Project-Specific Requirements

Material-specific requirements may involve ACI 318 for structural concrete and AISC 360 for structural steel, with AISC 341 applicable to certain seismic steel systems. ASCE 41 may be relevant to existing-building evaluation and retrofit.

Healthcare projects may involve HCAI requirements, while FEMA technical guidance, ASTM specifications, UL qualifications, manufacturer requirements, and project specifications may apply in specific circumstances.

No single standard should be assumed to govern every project. The engineering team must establish the applicable requirements based on location, code edition, building use, structural system, scope, AHJ, and project specifications.
